Health Information Judgement & Decisions

Two related analyses of social-media health-information judgement and self-reported health-decision use: one within 2024 and one comparing independent survey cycles in 2022 and 2024.

Research-development work · Observational findings · Not a peer-reviewed or AI-specific study

Research Sequence

One problem, two research questions

HINTS 7 · 2024

Is difficulty judging truthfulness associated with reported health-decision use?

1.83

Adjusted odds ratio (95% CI 1.42–2.34); complete-case n=5,183.

Cross-sectional survey-weighted logistic regression using the HINTS person weight and 50 JK1 replicate weights. Association does not establish causation.

Methods and validation
HINTS 6 + 7 · 2022/2024

Did the association differ between independent survey cycles?

1.20

Survey-year interaction OR (95% CI 0.83–1.73); p=0.330; pooled n=10,838.

Repeated cross-sectional model with 100 merged Rizzo replicate weights and 98 design degrees of freedom. No established change in association.

Methods and validation
Interpretation boundary: the adjusted estimate from HINTS 7 and the survey-year interaction estimate from HINTS 6+7 answer different questions and use different analytic samples. They cannot be interpreted as two estimates of the same effect.

The descriptive gap is numerically larger in 2024, but the formal survey-year interaction is not statistically significant (p=0.330). A within-person change cannot be inferred from independent cross-sectional samples.

Methodological Interpretation

Decisions worth examining

Sampling and uncertainty

Population survey weights and replicate-based standard errors were used instead of treating observations as a simple random sample. The pooled survey follows NCI merging-tool logic.

Measurement

Perceived difficulty judging social-media health information is measured with a single survey item. It should not be presented as a validated measure of digital health literacy.

Causal inference

Neither design establishes that judging difficulty causes information use. Temporality, residual confounding and reporting effects remain unresolved.

Next research question

Which information-judgement processes could explain this association, and how might a prospective or experimental design distinguish the possible explanations?

These analyses do not measure how people respond to AI-generated health advice. Any extension to that question would require distinct data and a new design.
