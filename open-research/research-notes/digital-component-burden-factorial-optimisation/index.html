When Does an Added Digital Component Become a Burden?

Factorial optimisation, engagement and the limits of attributing weight outcomes to component enactment

A paper-level working interpretation of research by Gina Wren (University of Oxford). Pending Catherine review; no author contact or endorsement implied.

A — digital intervention mechanisms (weight management, not physical activity)

Primary literature and versions

Original evidence

Wren GM, Koutoukidis DA, Scragg J, et al. Optimising a digitally delivered behavioural weight loss programme: a factorial cluster randomised controlled trial. npj Digital Medicine. Published online 19 September 2026. DOI: 10.1038/s41746-026-03231-y.

Peer-reviewed early online version; publisher indicates final Version of Record pending.
Study design

What was actually studied

A 24 factorial trial of four added components in a commercial weight-management programme: introductory coaching call, coaching webchat, goal-setting statements and food-diary review plus feedback. A total of 1,335 adults were allocated to 16 experimental conditions. The primary endpoint was weight change at 16 weeks, with additional weight, programme drop-out and engagement outcomes.

Reported evidence

What the paper found

At 16 weeks the introductory call showed no detected effect; at 24 weeks it was associated with about 1 kg greater weight loss (p=0.002), but that finding did not persist in the per-protocol analysis. The food-diary feedback component was associated with less weight loss at 24 weeks (+0.74 kg relative difference, p=0.02; the 16-week +0.52 kg result had p=0.07). These results do not demonstrate that all feedback is harmful, nor that participants' enactment of a specific BCT caused a weight change.

Methodological reading

Distinguishing availability, exposure and outcome

A component can be assigned, delivered, opened, understood and enacted at different rates. Factorial randomisation principally estimates effects of assigned components under the programme's actual delivery conditions. Per-protocol exposure comparisons are vulnerable to selection unless their assumptions and estimand are made explicit. Increased digital complexity could entail extra effort, but the trial alone does not isolate such burden as the causal mechanism.

Question for author correction

A specific methodological question

When optimising a factorial digital behaviour-change intervention, what minimum component-level exposure and enactment data would allow investigators to distinguish a genuinely unhelpful assigned feature from burden or selective non-use? Should component assignment effects remain the primary estimands, with secondary receipt-based analyses framed as sensitivity analyses rather than equivalent causal effects?

This is a proposed question, not a claim that the original authors overlooked this limitation or have agreed to collaborate.

Connection to Catherine

Transfer to her research assets

Catherine's BCT enactment and active-comparator notes raise an analogous measurement distinction for digital physical-activity interventions: “included in the package” differs from “used and enacted.” This trial tests weight-management outcomes, not physical activity or AI health advice.
