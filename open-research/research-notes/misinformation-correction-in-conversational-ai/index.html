From Static Corrections to Conversational AI: What Is the Treatment?

Reconsidering misinformation exposure, expert authority and repeated correction

An experimental-design question arising from Santosh Vijaykumar's UK–Brazil WhatsApp misinformation study and a later expert-voices research agenda.

Health misinformationCorrection experimentsConversational AIExpert trustBehavioural outcomes
Empirical anchor

A randomized exposure study separates several shades of misinformation

Vijaykumar S, Jin Y, Rogerson D, et al. How shades of truth and age affect responses to COVID-19 (Mis)information: randomized survey experiment among WhatsApp users in UK and Brazil. Humanities and Social Sciences Communications. 2021;8:88. Read open-access article.

Two online experiments in the UK and Brazil enrolled 1,454 adult WhatsApp users in total. Participants were randomized to one of three initial messages about garlic as a supposed COVID-19 remedy: full falsity, partial falsity or truth. They then all saw the same WHO corrective message, with beliefs, perceived credibility and intention to share measured before and after the corrective exposure.

PopulationUK and Brazil WhatsApp users; combined N=1,454
Randomized factorInitial message: false, partially false, or true
Correction phaseAll participants shown the same WHO message
Measured outcomesBeliefs, perceived credibility and sharing intention

The authors reported higher susceptibility to misinformation beliefs among younger adults in both settings and a possible backfire pattern among older UK respondents after corrective exposure. They also describe improved endorsement and intention to share accurate information after correction.

Design limitation

The correction phase was not itself a correction-versus-no-correction randomized comparison

randomized initial message type → first beliefs / credibility / sharing-intention rating → SAME WHO correction for all → second rating

Because every group received a correction at the second stage, change between the two ratings cannot cleanly identify a standalone causal effect of receiving correction versus not receiving it. Repeated measurement, the identity of the claim and source cues are potential components of the observed response. The study's randomized claim-type comparison remains informative within this design.

Inference boundary: the experiments did not use generative AI, and they did not isolate correction content, WHO-source attribution and message repetition in independent randomized arms.
Recent conceptual context

Expert authority may have a different role in conversational settings

Lu X, Jin Y, Vijaykumar S. Overcoming informational conflict: The role, limits, and new opportunities of expert voices in crisis and risk communication. Crisis and Risk Communication. 2025;1(1–2):122–135. DOI: 10.1080/29986907.2025.2580989.

This later article synthesizes challenges facing expert-led misinformation correction and proposes research directions under conditions of informational conflict. It is a conceptual paper rather than an AI-chatbot effectiveness trial.

A conversational agent can repeat misinformation during clarification, summarize a correction, provide citations, and shift the apparent origin of authority between the model and external experts. Those attributes may alter what users perceive as the authoritative source of a claim.

A new experimental unit

Does correction happen at the message, conversation or verification step?

misleading claim introduced → AI responds across several turns → expert evidence cited (or not) → user verifies (or not) → belief / confidence / intention → downstream health decision

A single static-message experiment treats corrective exposure as a discrete event. In conversational AI, exposure is sequential and potentially personalized. There may be no single corrective “message” once the user asks follow-up questions or requests conflicting advice.

Candidate study

Separate information accuracy, source attribution and conversation structure

Design componentWhy distinguish it?
Correction factual contentTests evidence-based explanation rather than generic rebuttal
Source attributionSeparates WHO / clinician provenance from AI-only assertions
Interaction formatStatic card versus multi-turn dialogue with the same core evidence
Repeated claim exposureAssesses whether repetition contributes to familiarity or misbelief
Measured behaviourCross-checking, consultation or action choice in addition to sharing intention

A factorial or sequentially randomized design, with a suitable comparison condition, could help distinguish these factors. It would need to avoid unnecessarily exposing participants to harmful misinformation and establish a defensible debrief and ethics protocol.

Competing explanations

What else could explain an apparent source or correction effect?

Differences in perceived credibility might reflect comprehension, health knowledge, motivated reasoning, trust in institutions, literacy, prompt phrasing, model verbosity, or the number of times a claim is repeated. A single-topic vignette could overstate how consistently effects generalize across medical topics.

Open methodological question

What exactly should be randomized in an AI correction trial?

When misinformation and correction unfold across multiple turns of a conversational AI system, what is the appropriate unit of corrective exposure—a statement, the whole conversation, an attributed expert source, or the user's opportunity to verify? Would independently randomizing corrective content, expert-source attribution and conversational format, while measuring actual verification or health-action choices, address a mechanism gap that the static WHO-correction paradigm cannot resolve?

This is a proposed extension of the research programme, not a claim that conversational AI was evaluated in either cited article.

Method boundary

Interpretive research-development note

The 2021 study predates contemporary generative AI interfaces and examines simulated WhatsApp messages about one COVID-19 misinformation claim. The 2025 work is a conceptual synthesis rather than an experiment. Neither study supports an estimated causal effect of AI-mediated misinformation correction. Corrections from the authors and other health-communication researchers are welcome.
