Does a Misinformation-Induced Trust Loss Stay Local?

Local biobank trust, wider science attitudes and the boundary of experimental inference.

Paper-level reading for a possible supervisor-oriented research conversation with John Kerr · University of Otago. This is independent interpretation; no author contact or endorsement has occurred.

Health misinformationScientific trustRandomized exposureMechanism
Primary empirical source

The paper being interpreted

Marques MD, Kerr JR, Stukas AA, McLennan J. The effect of scientific conspiracy theories on scepticism towards biotechnologies. Public Understanding of Science. 2025;34(5):646–664. Online 4 December 2024. Read original study / DOI.

Researcher's official university / centre page

Verified evidence

Design, sample, comparisons and outcomes

DesignThree preregistered online experiments with random assignment to pro-conspiracy, conspiracy/debunking or neutral text conditions.
SampleAustralian adult online participants: n = 309, 343 and 348; combined N = 1,000.
OutcomesConspiracy endorsement, intentions to eat GM food, support for biobanks, trust in biobank scientists, broader science-populist beliefs and GM policy support.
Public materialsPreregistration and study materials, data and code listed by authors at https://osf.io/5eh8b/.

Exposure increased endorsement of specific conspiracy beliefs. Study 1 found a small effect on intention to eat GM food. Study 2 reported reduced policy support for biobanks and an association with lower trust in biobank scientists; Study 3 did not find the corresponding effect on broader science-populist attitudes or distant GM policy support. The authors caution that more distal effects were small or nonsignificant and that immediate exposure in a relatively educated Australian online sample limits inference.

Evidence boundary

What this study cannot identify

The study does not test AI-generated health information, real clinical behaviour, delayed persistence or generalized loss of trust in all science. Statistical mediation in Study 2 does not independently randomize the mediator, so the mechanism is not experimentally isolated.
Catherine's interpretation

A testable mechanism extension

A misinformation narrative may lower perceived trustworthiness of its named researchers without propagating to an abstract belief about science institutions. Transfer to AI-delivered health information would require new measurement, not simple extrapolation.

Competing explanations

Differences across studies could also reflect changes in the target construct and stimulus, outcome measurement, population characteristics or sampling variation, not only distance through a trust pathway.

Author-correctable question

What should the next analysis distinguish?

Would a follow-up randomized study separating trust in the specific scientists named in a misinformation narrative from trust in scientists generally, with delayed measurement and an observed fact-checking choice, clarify whether Study 2's apparent local trust pathway fails to extend to Study 3's distal outcomes because of construct distance or because the effect does not persist?

The question is offered for the authors' correction. It does not claim that the authors have endorsed the proposed extension.
