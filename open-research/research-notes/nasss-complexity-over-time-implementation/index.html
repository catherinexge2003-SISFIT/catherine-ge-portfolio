When Does Complexity Explain Digital Health Non-Adoption?

Turning cross-domain implementation explanations into traceable longitudinal observations

A paper-level methodological reading of research by Trisha Greenhalgh (University of Oxford). Pending Catherine review. No author participation or endorsement is implied.

A / B methods — digital health implementation and complex systems

Verified bibliography

Primary published evidence

Greenhalgh T, Wherton J, Papoutsi C, et al. Analysing the role of complexity in explaining the fortunes of technology programmes: empirical application of the NASSS framework. BMC Medicine. 2018;16:66. DOI: 10.1186/s12916-018-1050-6.

Version of Record, 2018-05-14.
Study design

What the authors actually studied

The first empirical application of the NASSS framework investigated six technology-supported programmes using ethnography and action research for up to three years across 20 health/care organisations and 10 national-level bodies. Technologies included video consultations, remote heart-failure biomarker monitoring, safety systems and case-management software.

Results

What their evidence supports

The study uses cases to demonstrate how multiple interacting dimensions of complexity can constrain adoption, scale-up, spread and sustainability. It is qualitative and theory-informed; it does not provide a randomized effect of a particular technology or assign one causal weight to each NASSS domain.

Methodological reading

A distinction for follow-up research

A multi-domain complexity explanation can become circular if a project is labelled “complex” only after implementation fails. Longitudinal evidence should distinguish baseline conditions, changing service configurations, adaptations and outcome sequences. Static checklists may under-represent interactions and feedback loops across organisations and time.

Author-correctable question

A specific question worth testing

In a longitudinal NASSS-based evaluation, what minimum time-stamped observations would allow analysts to test rival explanations of a programme's non-adoption or abandonment rather than retrospectively assigning complexity labels to an observed failure? Would prespecifying a small set of cross-domain transitions (such as workflow change → feature availability → patient access → use) improve interpretability without flattening the dynamic systems view?

This question invites correction and alternative study designs; it does not assert that the authors overlooked the point.

Connection to Catherine

Why this study matters to her research scope

This builds upon Catherine's Papoutsi NHS App note. The distinct contribution is temporal attribution and competing explanations across NASSS domains, rather than a second note about whether a single app feature was enabled.

Limitations

What the study does not show

The six programmes are heterogeneous health-service technologies, not physical-activity interventions. Empirical cases can inform plausible explanations, but cannot by themselves identify causal contributions of individual features or confirm an AI advice effect.
