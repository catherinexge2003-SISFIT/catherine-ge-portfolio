From Conditional AI Acceptance to a Real Data-Sharing Decision

What does interview-based social licence identify about actual health-AI adoption?

Paper-level reading for a possible supervisor-oriented research conversation with Rosie Dobson / Robyn Whittaker · University of Auckland TRANSFORM. This is independent interpretation; no author contact or endorsement has occurred.

AI in healthcarePatient trustImplementationConsent
Primary empirical source

The paper being interpreted

Dobson R, Stowell M, Tane T, Style R, Hiini K, Whittaker R. End-user perspectives on the use of personal health information for AI development and the use of AI in care: an interview study. Journal of Primary Health Care. 2026. Read original study / DOI.

Researcher's official university / centre page

Verified evidence

Design, sample, comparisons and outcomes

DesignCross-sectional, semi-structured, scenario-based interviews with rapid assessment procedure (RAP) analysis.
Sample51 interviewees: 21 health professionals, 17 patients, and 13 people undergoing routine screening.
Exposure / taskHypothetical scenarios about secondary use of personal health information and AI in clinical care.
OutcomesInterview themes and contextual conditions for acceptance; no measured live consent, uptake or clinical outcome.

Respondents broadly accepted potential uses of patient data for AI when associated with public benefit, privacy, transparent consent, human oversight and strong governance. The authors caution against one-size-fits-all social licence, acknowledge limited diversity among some population groups and highlight RAP's breadth-over-depth trade-off. They report further work on consent and transparency for specific AI use cases.

Evidence boundary

What this study cannot identify

The 51 interviews identify expressed perceptions under scenarios; these are not a randomised comparison, observed real-world data authorisation, or evidence that any AI tool improves health outcomes. Māori data sovereignty and governance cannot be treated as generic consent preferences.
Catherine's interpretation

A testable mechanism extension

Stated acceptance may be conditional on the identity of the data recipient and a named clinician remaining accountable. A prospective decision design could separate institutional trust from a willingness to authorize a bounded secondary-data use.

Competing explanations

A gap between expressed acceptance and consent may reflect scenario comprehension, personal data sensitivity, institutional history, the irreversibility of data sharing or the specific clinical context, not necessarily inconsistency or a literacy deficit.

Author-correctable question

What should the next analysis distinguish?

In the team's next consent-and-transparency studies, what observable choice would separate conditional social licence expressed during an AI-use-case interview from a decision to authorise bounded use of one's own data? Would experimentally varying clinician accountability and data recipient, then measuring a consequential low-risk consent choice, preserve the equity and governance concerns identified here?

The question is offered for the authors' correction. It does not claim that the authors have endorsed the proposed extension.
