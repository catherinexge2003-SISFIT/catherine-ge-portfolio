From Co-Designed Requirements to Self-Care Enactment

When do digital features derived from COM-B become independently testable self-care behaviours?

A paper-level working interpretation of research by Rebecca Nourse (University of Oxford). Pending Catherine review; no author contact or endorsement implied.

Primary literature and versions

Original evidence

Nourse R, Kayser L, Maddison R. Developing Requirements for a Digital Self-Care Intervention for Adults With Heart Failure: Qualitative Workshop Study. J Med Internet Res. 2025;27:e72589. DOI: 10.2196/72589.

Published journal Version of Record 2025-08-25; earlier JMIR preprint posted February 2025.
Study design

What was actually studied

The SmartHeart development study combined four co-design workshops with the Behaviour Change Wheel, COM-B, TDF and user-centred requirements analysis. There were 16 adults with heart failure and four informal caregivers. This was qualitative intervention-requirements development, not a trial testing health outcomes.

Reported evidence

What the paper found

Researchers mapped barriers across all three COM-B domains and 11 of 14 TDF domains into 28 user needs. Participants generated six proposed intervention-component themes (the “what”), and six user-requirement themes describing operation and usability (the “how”). The development process bridges barriers to specification, but the study did not show that these functions were implemented, encountered or causally effective.

Methodological reading

Distinguishing availability, exposure and outcome

An evidence trail could link each identified user need to an intended component, a specific task in the prototype, direct evidence of attempted/enacted self-care, and relevant longer-term outcome. A co-designed requirement can be legitimate even when no quantitative outcome is available; premature claims of mechanism efficacy would be unwarranted.

Question for author correction

A specific methodological question

How should researchers preserve the traceability of COM-B-derived user needs through successive prototype iterations so that later feasibility studies can distinguish a requirement being implemented, a function being used, and a self-care action being enacted? Which of these transitions are best evidenced by direct task observation versus user experience accounts?

This is a proposed question, not a claim that the original authors overlooked this limitation or have agreed to collaborate.

Connection to Catherine

Transfer to her research assets

This extends Catherine's existing BCT-enactment and physical-activity-adherence work to intervention requirements. Nourse's study involves heart-failure self-care, not a demonstrated physical-activity adherence effect.

Evidence boundary

What cannot be inferred

Participants were from Australian clinical/community contexts; a small purposive qualitative sample is not a nationally representative estimate. Features remained proposed design requirements at this stage. Rebecca Nourse is now affiliated with Oxford, but the work was conducted with Deakin University and the University of Copenhagen.
