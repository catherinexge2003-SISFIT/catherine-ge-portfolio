When an AI Relays Health Advice, Whose Trust Does a Survey Measure?

An S-TIMHSS measurement question about the referent of trust in AI-mediated health communication.

Paper-level reading for a possible supervisor-oriented research conversation with Samantha Meyer · University of Waterloo. This is independent interpretation; no author contact or endorsement has occurred.

Health communicationPsychometricsAI-mediated trust
Primary empirical source

The paper being interpreted

Meyer SB, Little J, Ward PR, Brown P, Calnan M. Development and validation of the S-TIMHSS: a quality metric to inform and evaluate interventions to (re)build trust. Frontiers in Public Health. 2025;13:1568836. Read original study / DOI.

Researcher's official university / centre page

Verified evidence

Design, sample, comparisons and outcomes

DesignA Canadian survey conducted in September 2024; cross-sectional item-reduction and scale-validation work.
SampleN = 512 survey respondents; this is not a generative-AI intervention sample.
Comparisons38-item TIMHSS was shortened to an 18-item, three-domain S-TIMHSS (doctor, health system, health policy).
OutcomesFactor-model evaluation, convergent/discriminant and criterion validity, with measures including self-reported intended health-related behaviour.

The instrument supports more practical measurement of several forms of healthcare trust. The article explicitly notes that respondents may have different doctors in mind: their own provider, a specialist, or physicians generally. It also calls for test–retest evidence, additional invariance work, and testing in healthcare systems beyond Canada.

Evidence boundary

What this study cannot identify

The study validates an 18-item health-system trust measure in a Canadian sample. It neither validates the instrument for AI advice nor establishes a causal effect of improving trust on health behaviour. Associations with intentions are not observed adherence.
Catherine's interpretation

A testable mechanism extension

An AI assistant can be the proximate messenger while authority is attributed to a doctor, a public institution or the model itself. The user may report confidence in the messenger, the cited expert or the institution differently. This is an extension hypothesis, not an S-TIMHSS finding.

Competing explanations

Any difference could arise from different perceived accountability, familiarity with a personal clinician, prior healthcare experiences, or interpretation of the item wording, even with identical advice quality.

Author-correctable question

What should the next analysis distinguish?

When a user receives health advice from an AI assistant citing a clinician, a health service or a government source, which trustee is represented by an S-TIMHSS response? Would a provenance-vignette study and measurement-invariance / differential-item-functioning check be needed before comparing those scores with clinician-only advice?

The question is offered for the authors' correction. It does not claim that the authors have endorsed the proposed extension.
