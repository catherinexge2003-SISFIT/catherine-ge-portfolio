Does Digital Health Literacy Measure AI Advice Judgement?

From perceived eHealth skills to observable decisions about generated health claims

A measurement-oriented Research Note on a 2026 UK digital-health-literacy survey coauthored by Ana Luísa Neves at Imperial College London.

UK population surveyeHEALSAI health adviceMeasurementHealth equity
Empirical anchor

A UK internet-user survey identifies correlates of self-reported digital health literacy

Exton-Smith H, Sousi S, Alharbi A, El-Osta A, Greenfield G, Hayhoe B, Majeed A, Neves AL. Factors Associated With Digital Health Literacy in the United Kingdom: Cross-Sectional Online Survey. Journal of Medical Internet Research. 2026;28:e89136. DOI: 10.2196/89136.

The authors analysed an online survey of 1,525 adult UK internet users, fielded via YouGov in November–December 2024. Digital health literacy was measured with eight self-reported eHEALS items (range 8–40); 21% fell below the pre-specified threshold of 26. The study models associations with demographic, educational, economic and social variables.

Older age, lower social grade, lower educational attainment, sex and social-contact patterns were associated with lower self-reported scores in the authors' main model. Associations are not causal effects; the threshold and sample inclusion criteria shape the reported prevalence.

Interpretation boundary

eHEALS is self-assessed capability, not observed verification performance

The authors themselves caution that eHEALS focuses on perceived confidence or self-efficacy rather than demonstrated skills. High internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha 0.93 in this sample) does not establish ability to recognise an unsupported AI-generated medical recommendation.

Key distinction: perceived digital health literacy, objectively observed health-information appraisal, and the willingness to verify AI-generated advice are related constructs, not interchangeable outcomes.

This is a prospective measurement question. The 2024 data collection did not measure AI-generated advice appraisal or AI hallucination detection.

The AI interface problem

What changes when the user no longer sees a set of candidate sources?

Self-reported ability to find information → confidence in appraisal ≠ observed accuracy on AI-generated claims ≠ appropriately calibrated reliance ≠ safer health action

A conventional web page often makes the source visible. A synthesized AI answer may be persuasive even when it does not expose sufficient evidence provenance. Conversely, users with less confidence may successfully cross-check an answer. A high self-rating need not equal sound judgment.

There is also an equity question: do apparent differences across age, education or social grade persist when measured with actual, standardized tasks rather than confidence ratings?

Proposed measurement extension

Separate what people think they can do from what they demonstrably do

ConstructPossible measure
Perceived literacyOriginal eight-item eHEALS scale; preserve comparability
AI claim discriminationIdentify true, unsupported and incorrect statements within matched health advice vignettes
Provenance interpretationIdentify whether cited sources actually support the answer
Trust calibrationCompare confidence with accuracy and willingness to verify
Action selectionChoose whether to use advice, consult a professional, or seek further evidence

A pragmatic next step would pair the established self-report measure with performance tasks featuring matched conventional web extracts and AI-style answers, with randomly varied accuracy and source transparency. Measurement-invariance and predictive-validity analyses could test whether the same self-reported score has similar meaning across formats and groups.

Competing explanations

The gap might not be uniquely caused by generative AI

Any discrepancy between confidence and performance could reflect general self-efficacy, numeracy, topic familiarity, language, vignette difficulty or access to relevant evidence—not specifically AI. Synthetic stimuli may also fail to reproduce real interactive behaviour. These issues require pilot testing and careful validation.

Open methodological question

Should AI health literacy include a performance test?

Given that the UK survey uses self-reported eHEALS, would you view performance-based appraisal of AI-generated health advice—especially unsupported-claim detection, source verification and confidence calibration—as a distinct outcome alongside digital health literacy? Could a short standardized task battery linked to eHEALS help test whether sociodemographic gradients in perceived ability also appear in demonstrated judgment, without prematurely treating AI literacy as an entirely new construct?

This question concerns a possible future study, not a criticism that the UK survey promised to assess AI skills.

Method boundary

What can and cannot be inferred

The paper measures UK adult internet users rather than the entire UK population; it is cross-sectional, uses a particular cutpoint, and does not assess AI-specific competence or causal predictors. Ana Luísa Neves is a coauthor and supervised the work; Geva Greenfield is the corresponding author. This note does not attribute all study decisions to any one coauthor. Corrections are welcome.
