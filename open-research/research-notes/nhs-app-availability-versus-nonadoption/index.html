When App Features Are Unavailable, Is It Non-Adoption?

A measurement boundary in the implementation of the NHS App

A paper-level methodological reading of Reidy, Papoutsi et al. (2025), focused on variation in local service configuration, patient opportunity to use features, and what counts as non-adoption. Pending Catherine review.

Primary empirical source

What this study actually examined

Reidy C, Papoutsi C, KC S, Gudgin B, Laverty AA, Greaves F, Powell J. Qualitative evaluation of the implementation and national roll-out of the NHS App in England. BMC Medicine. 2025;23:20. Published 21 January 2025; version of record. DOI: 10.1186/s12916-024-03842-w. Reidy and Papoutsi contributed equally as first authors.

The NHS App provides a digital access route to health services, such as prescriptions, GP-related records, appointment booking when locally enabled, and the COVID Pass. It is not a trial of a physical-activity behaviour-change intervention. The study examines the longitudinal implementation and use of a national patient portal in heterogeneous primary-care systems.

Design and period

Longitudinal multi-method qualitative evaluation, June 2021–April 2023. Five GP practice sites in England, with documentary analysis and cross-site comparisons.

Participants

88 participants: 62 interviews involving 66 individuals plus four focus groups involving 22 patients/carers, including users and non-users, staff and stakeholders.

Additional data

Approximately 60 hours of practice-site observation; think-aloud interviews with four patients within the interview sample; review of approximately 100 documents.

Analytical framework

NASSS (Non-adoption, Abandonment, Scale-up, Spread and Sustainability); thematic analysis combining inductive and deductive coding across its domains.

These qualitative observations describe mechanisms and implementation experiences; the study does not estimate a randomized causal effect on health behaviour or a national feature-specific uptake rate.

Observed pattern

Availability was partly determined outside the user's control

Although the NHS App appeared to offer a uniform national interface, the authors show that its practical functions depended on each GP practice's technical integrations, workflow decisions and staff capacity. Appointment booking was disabled at some practices, sometimes because of clinical triage requirements rather than because users were unwilling or unable to use it.

Patients receiving much of their care in secondary or community services sometimes found incomplete records and limited utility in the GP-centred portal. Other participants valued prescription ordering or COVID Pass functionality. The study also identifies registration friction, digital inclusion issues, uneven organisational support, and differences in health-system literacy.

The application evolved during the study. This makes feature availability a site-by-time property, not a fixed characteristic of the technology or a fixed opportunity shared by all participants.

Analytical problem

Three distinct reasons for a missing use event

feature exists in the national product ↓ feature is enabled and appropriate in this GP pathway ↓ eligible patient can locate and access the feature ↓ patient attempts a relevant task ↓ task is completed and useful for care

A recorded absence of use can arise because the feature is locally disabled, irrelevant to a patient's clinical pathway, inaccessible given their circumstances, difficult to discover or use, or actively declined. Collapsing these into one 'non-adoption' category may misattribute organisational configuration and patient eligibility to an individual-level technology acceptance problem.

Interpretive boundary: The original article already describes local functionality differences and access barriers. This note does not claim the authors overlooked them. It develops a proposed measurement protocol for a follow-up implementation study.
Operational extension

How a follow-up study might define its denominator

Practice × feature × time

Record which functions are actually enabled at each site and in each study period, with reasons for disabling (including workflow and clinical constraints).

Opportunity and eligibility

For a given use case, distinguish eligible patients with an opportunity to use the feature from all nominal app registrants. Do not assume a feature is appropriate for every patient.

Task-level evidence

Combine sampled patient-task observation or consented interaction evidence with practice workflow data, rather than inferring purposeful use from registration alone.

Interpretation and ethics

Qualitative explanations can complement task outcomes and address accessibility; linking transaction logs requires privacy, proportionality and data-governance safeguards.

This design would distinguish opportunity to use from successful use, without treating all differences in feature uptake as patient deficits. It is a prospective proposal, not a result estimated in the 2025 study.

Author-correctable question

When is 'non-adoption' a valid outcome?

In a longitudinal evaluation of a nationally distributed patient portal whose features are enabled, disabled or adapted locally, what is the most defensible denominator for non-adoption of a particular feature? Would a practice × feature × time availability record, combined with purposive patient-task observations, help distinguish non-opportunity-to-use from non-use of an available and relevant service? What would such a design risk overlooking about clinical appropriateness, evolving service workflows and patient experience?

This question seeks methodological correction and alternative measurement strategies; it is not an invitation or response from the paper's authors.

Connection to Catherine's research

Where the methods transfer—and where they do not

Catherine's work on digital physical-activity intervention engagement and behaviour-change technique enactment raises a related measurement question: a support feature coded as present does not ensure that a participant was offered, encountered or meaningfully used it. The NHS App evidence illustrates the importance of institutional pathways and service constraints upstream of exposure.

Transfer should remain at the level of implementation and measurement design. The NHS App paper does not demonstrate that exercise adherence improved, that BCTs were enacted, or that AI-mediated health advice changed real decisions. Those would require separate empirical designs and outcome measures.

Limits of this evidence

What cannot be inferred

Qualitative sampling across five GP practices does not establish representative uptake percentages or population-wide causal effects. NHS App versions and features changed rapidly; patient experiences may reflect particular configurations and pandemic-era context. The authors report limited engagement with some groups experiencing language or other disadvantages. Proposed denominators and instruments are not validated in this article.
