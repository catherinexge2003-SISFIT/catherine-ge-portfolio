What Remains of eHealth Literacy When AI Hides the Search?

From locating web resources to evaluating a single synthesized health answer

A methodological reading of Marco Bardus's eHEALS study, extended cautiously to his 2026 discussion of generative AI and health information seeking.

Digital health literacyAI health informationMeasurement validityHealth communicationBehaviour change
Empirical anchor

The original paper measures perceived ability to find, appraise and use online health information

Bardus M, Keriabian A, Elbejjani M, Al-Hajj S. Assessing eHealth literacy among internet users in Lebanon: A cross-sectional study. DIGITAL HEALTH. 2022;8. DOI: 10.1177/20552076221119336.

A cross-sectional web survey recruited 2,715 adults residing in Lebanon, mostly via social platforms and printed outreach. Among 2,336 respondents completing all eight eHEALS items, the mean score was approximately 28.8/40 (SD 5.5). The study examined reliability, possible factor structures and associations between self-reported eHealth literacy, internet use and demographic variables.

Sample2,715 respondents; 2,336 complete eHEALS assessments
InstrumentEight self-reported eHEALS items about internet health resources
ScoreMean about 28.8/40 among completers
DesignCross-sectional; nonprobability recruitment and self-report

The study already recognises that the eHEALS instrument was created for an earlier, more static internet and discusses concerns about its factor structure. It does not assume the instrument is beyond revision.

Current research context

Generative AI changes which information-seeking steps are visible

NIHR School for Public Health Research, 18 March 2026. Dr Marco Bardus, Generative AI and health information seeking. Webinar programme.

The seminar programme discusses how generative AI changes searching and interpreting health information, including risks from inaccurate or outdated responses, overconfidence, and plausible but unsupported statements. This is a current research context, not an empirical trial or evidence that a new literacy scale has been validated.

Traditional search can expose multiple sources, snippets, links and publication cues. A conversational answer may compress that process into one coherent response. The user's ability to assess evidence may remain important even when the interface no longer asks them to search directly.

Construct question

An apparent reduction in search work is not necessarily an increase in literacy

Classic web path: search → inspect sources → compare → appraise → apply
Conversational-AI path: ask → receive synthesis → check source/provenance? → calibrate trust → apply

Some eHEALS items concern awareness of where to find and how to evaluate health resources; others concern confidence in using information for decisions. If an AI interface performs retrieval and synthesis on behalf of a user, the meaning of self-reported “finding” or “evaluating” skills may shift.

Interpretation, not a finding: a valid pre-AI self-report measure may remain useful, but its scores alone cannot demonstrate whether a person successfully identifies unsupported claims in an AI answer.
Testable design

How a future study could test construct continuity rather than assume it

Measurement layerObservable evidence
Perceived skillTraditional eHEALS subscales or total score
Source appraisal performanceCorrect identification of evidence quality in matched web-result tasks
AI answer appraisalDetection of supported versus unsupported claims in standardized AI-style advice
Confidence calibrationWhether confidence tracks actual correctness, not only self-belief
Behavioural transferWhether participants cross-check before making a consequential health decision

One possible study would randomize interface format (search-results page versus conversational synthesis) while holding the underlying health question and evidence quality constant. It could compare how eHEALS relates to task performance in each interface. Item-level and measurement-invariance analyses might help distinguish interface effects from genuine differences in the latent construct.

Alternative explanations

What could complicate a simplistic AI-literacy claim

Differences may arise from prior health knowledge, language proficiency, AI familiarity, education, domain-specific stakes, interface design, time constraints, and whether sources are verifiable. In particular, failures to check AI claims may reflect missing access to source material rather than deficient literacy.

The Lebanon survey's recruitment, context and time period are not representative of today's UK AI users. Its original estimates should not be exported to a new population.

Open methodological question

Which literacy skills survive when source comparison is hidden?

If conversational AI supplies a synthesized answer without requiring the user to locate and compare source pages, which dimensions of the eHEALS “find, appraise and use” structure would you expect to retain predictive validity for competent health-information evaluation? Which dimensions require separate, performance-based measures of provenance checking, unsupported-claim detection and calibrated trust? What is the smallest study that could distinguish a change in literacy from a change in interface affordances?

The question invites correction about both measurement theory and study design; it does not assume eHEALS has already failed in the AI setting.

Method boundary

A research-development interpretation, not a validation study

This note links a 2022 cross-sectional eHealth-literacy study to a 2026 publicly described research topic. The 2022 participants were not tested on generative AI, and the 2026 webinar is not a controlled experiment. Neither proves that eHEALS has lost validity for AI-mediated health advice. Corrections from the authors are welcome.
